UFC Fight Winner Predictor

A logistic-regression model that predicts the winner of a UFC fight from each fighter's pre-fight record — striking, grappling, form, and a rating system. This is a hypothesis-driven account of building it: the data, the 69 features, why the winning model won and what its coefficients actually say, and every result — including what was tried and didn't work.

Sourced from ufcstats.com's official fight statistics, via the Greco1899/scrape_ufc_stats open dataset — three tables (fighters, fights, rounds) shipped in the repo's data/ directory so results are exactly reproducible from a clone.

Every fight is used from both fighters' perspectives — a bout between A and B produces one training row as "A minus B" and one as "B minus A," with the win label flipped accordingly. This makes the dataset perfectly symmetric (confirmed: win rate is exactly 50.00%) and removes any "listed first" bias.

Draws and no-contests are excluded — there is no winner to predict. A small number of fighters (8 identified) share an identical display name in the source data; see Limitations.

Model & Training

Logistic regression, calibrated, over a tuned ELO

Four candidate models were fit on the training period and scored on validation AUC. Logistic regression won clearly — the differential feature design gives it a close-to-linear decision boundary that tree ensembles couldn't beat, even tuned:

ModelValidation AUC
Logistic Regression0.7148selected
XGBoost0.6847
Random Forest0.6838
Hist Gradient Boosting0.6786

The winning model's raw scores are isotonic-calibrated on the validation period (not Platt scaling — the base scores were overconfident at the extremes, which a monotonic sigmoid can't correct but a non-parametric isotonic map can), so a "70%" from this model means what it says.

The ELO system's own update rate — how much a single result moves a fighter's rating — is tuned the same way: swept from K=32 to K=320 in a validation-only search, landing on K=256. That value is persisted to a config file so the serving code and the training code are provably using the identical rating system, not two that have quietly drifted apart.

Why It Won

The features are built for a linear model — the results confirm it

Hypothesis. Every feature here is constructed as fighter A minus fighter B specifically so a linear decision function can exploit it: for sigmoid(Σ wᵢ·diffᵢ), swapping A and B negates every input, which negates the sum, which flips the prediction to exactly 1 − p. That symmetry is guaranteed by the math, not learned from data. A tree ensemble sees the same antisymmetric features but has no such guarantee — it has to discover the symmetry empirically, and with roughly 7,150 independent fights to learn from, it may not have enough data to do that reliably. If the hypothesis is right, tree ensembles shouldn't just score lower on accuracy — they should be measurably less internally consistent.

Test. For every validation fight, each candidate model's prediction from fighter A's row was compared against 1 minus its prediction from fighter B's row for the same fight — before predict.py's averaging step has a chance to reconcile any difference.

ModelVal AUCMean self-disagreementFights >10% inconsistent
Logistic Regression0.71480.00080.0%
XGBoost0.68470.03895.9%

Hist Gradient Boosting0.67860.03393.5%

Result. Logistic regression's decision function is symmetric to four decimal places — accurate not by coincidence but because it structurally can't be otherwise. The tree ensembles are 10–50× less self-consistent: on a real share of fights, they'd give a genuinely different answer depending on which fighter happened to be listed first, and the CLI's averaging step is quietly reconciling two disagreeing opinions rather than confirming one coherent one. That's a structural reason to prefer the linear model, independent of the leaderboard.

What the winning model actually weights

Standardized logistic-regression coefficients — top 15 of 69 features, by |magnitude|

Most of this is intuitive: younger, higher-rated, better-defended, currently-winning fighters are favored, and cumulative damage absorbed drags the other way — all consistent with the reasons those features were added in the first place.

Read honestly, not cherry-picked

Three rows point the opposite of naive intuition: more career fights, more losses, and more tenure each independently push toward losing, while total fight-minutes pushes the other way. The likely explanation is multicollinearity, not a real effect: fight count, win count, and loss count are nearly linearly dependent (wins + losses ≈ fights), so a linear model can split credit between them in ways that look contradictory in isolation even though their combined effect is sensible. The same pattern shows up in title bouts fought (+0.155) vs. title bouts won (−0.117). Individual coefficients here shouldn't be read as isolated causal claims.

Why K needed retuning from 32 to 256. The original ELO lookup bug meant every rating read a fighter's state one fight before it should have — a permanently stale signal. Under that staleness, an aggressive K (fast-updating ratings) would have compounded the error: reacting quickly to information that was already outdated. Once the staleness was fixed, the rating became a genuinely fresh read of current form, and MMA specifically rewards weighting recent fights heavily — careers are short and styles evolve fight-to-fight, unlike chess where K=16–32 works because skill is stable for years. The validation-AUC curve peaking cleanly at K=256 rather than running away to K=∞ is the tell that this is a real signal, not an artifact — a miscalibrated search usually doesn't produce a well-behaved, unimodal optimum like that.

Isotonic over Platt, the honest version. The decisive evidence was a genuine held-out comparison on an earlier iteration of this model, before the strength-of-schedule and ELO fixes — where Platt scaling showed a real pathology (accuracy dropped in the highest confidence bucket, below the bucket just under it, a non-monotonic cliff a fixed-shape sigmoid can't correct), while isotonic's free-form monotonic map avoided it. Re-running that comparison on the current, much-improved model shows both methods behaving reasonably now — the underlying scores are simply better-behaved post-bugfix. Isotonic remains the right default on principle: it makes no assumption about the shape of miscalibration, which the fixed-shape alternative does.

Methodology

The discipline behind the number

A model's reported accuracy is only as honest as the process that produced it. Four rules were held throughout:

< 2024-01
train period
2024-01 → 2025-07
validation period
2025-07 →
test period, sealed
3×
total test-set evaluations, ever

No lookahead. A fighter's stats going into a given bout are computed strictly from fights before that date — verified directly by re-deriving a fighter's rolling stats from raw round data and checking every number to nine decimal places.

Model and hyperparameter choices never see the test set. Everything — which model wins, ELO's K, feature selection — is decided on the validation period alone. The test period has been evaluated exactly three times across this model's development: once for the original build, once after fixing two feature-computation bugs, once after adopting two validated improvements. Each was a genuine before/after comparison, not a search.

Train and serve run the same code. The command-line tool computes a live prediction with the identical functions used to build the training data — so the published accuracy is the accuracy of the tool, not of a lab-only pipeline.

What Was Tried

A 61-experiment validation sweep, plus an independent audit

Every idea below was scored on the validation period only, never the sealed test set. Two survived; the rest are recorded so they aren't tried again.

Adopted

ELO staleness bugfix
Training read a rating from the fighter's previous-to-last bout; serving read the current one — a genuine train/serve mismatch, not a tuning gap.
Strength-of-schedule zero-fill fix
Missing opponent-rating data was filled with 0.0 on an ELO scale centered at 1500 — manufacturing a ~1,500-point phantom gap on 15–30% of rows.
Physical-diff zero-fill fix
Missing height/reach/age produced a comparison against a phantom 0cm/0-year-old fighter instead of "no signal."
ELO K retuned, 32 → 256
The old K was optimal only for the buggy lookup; validation-AUC search found a real, well-behaved peak at K=256.
Opponent-quality table regenerated
The shipped strength-of-schedule data predated the ELO fix. Regenerated with the corrected ratings, plus a per-fighter "current state" row so live predictions aren't one fight stale.
Career damage-taken feature
Total significant strikes absorbed across a career — "chin mileage." +0.004 validation AUC alone.

Tested, not adopted

Gradient boosting / XGBoost, tuned
3+ points behind logistic regression even after a depth/learning-rate grid.
LR + GBM weighted ensembles
Best blend weight was 100% logistic regression — the ensemble added nothing.
Recency-weighted training (half-life 3–12y)
Every half-life tested reduced validation AUC.
Head-to-head record, common opponents
Sparse (only ~22% of fights share a common opponent) and net negative or flat.
Explicit southpaw indicator, age-curve terms
Both underperformed the existing stance-mismatch and linear age-diff features.
Dual-speed and RD-shrunk ELO variants
No gain over the single tuned-K rating.
Knockdown rates, round-by-round fade
Cardio-fade and knockdown signals measured ~zero effect, confirmed twice independently.
Explicit "is debut" indicator
Redundant with — and worse than — the existing career-count features.
Parked for a future batch

Average career fight duration (total octagon time ÷ fight count — separates fast finishers from decision grinders) measured +0.0035 validation AUC on its own, the single best untried idea found. Not yet adopted: folding it in now would mean a fourth look at the sealed test set for a small gain, which risks quietly overfitting the holdout rather than the model.

Limitations

What this model can't see

—

40% of fights are close to coin-flips even to the model (50–60% confidence band) — that's the honest variance in the sport, not a gap the data could close.

—

Eight fighters share an identical display name in the source data, which doesn't disambiguate them — for those, career stats may blend two separate people's fights.

—

No injuries, camps, weight cuts, or short-notice replacements — only what shows up in the box score.
